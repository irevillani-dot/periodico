# Fourteen kilometres apart

There are places that are inherently fascinating for International Relations aficionados. And there are places that a broad consensus considers overrated and not worth a visit. Gibraltar belongs to both categories. Since the urge to explore always beats trusting other people's experiences, in October I landed in Málaga and found myself heading towards Gibraltar, to the beat of *Brujería*.

*Brujería* (Spanish for witchcraft) is the word I would use to describe the magic of walking across a border from Spain into British territory and ending up in an English town: pubs, old-fashioned shop signs, the funniest mix of English and Spanish (they call it *llanito*), and a sheer limestone rock looming over everything. Besides the Barbary macaques lounging in the nature reserve, there is nothing to go crazy about. Gibraltar is essentially a military fortress, so you can visit installations, tunnels and forts.

The best view of the Strait is not from Gibraltar. Drive a little further and you reach the most scenic road in southern Spain. It ends in Tarifa, a kitesurfers' paradise and the southernmost point of the Iberian Peninsula.

On the other side lies Morocco. It is striking how close it is: at its narrowest the Strait is about 14 kilometres wide. And it is striking how much changes in those few kilometres. Spain's income per head is roughly eight times Morocco's, one of the widest gaps between neighbours anywhere. Fewer than one in five Moroccan women are in the labour market, against more than half in Spain, according to the World Bank. The landscape is dramatic. Jebel Musa, the mountain facing Tarifa, is nicknamed "the Dead Woman" because its silhouette looks like a woman lying on her back. On a clear day you can even spot the white houses of Ceuta, another headache for the Spanish government.

## The other border

Ceuta and Melilla, Spain's two cities on the African coast, are claimed by Morocco, which has never recognised Spanish sovereignty over them. But the issue that has shaped relations between Madrid and Rabat most in recent years lies 1,000 kilometres further south, in Western Sahara.

Western Sahara was a Spanish colony until 1975. That year, as Francisco Franco was dying, Morocco sent around 350,000 civilians across the border in the so-called Green March. Under the Madrid Accords, Spain handed the territory's administration over to Morocco and Mauritania and left in early 1976, without holding the referendum on self-determination the United Nations had called for. Morocco has controlled most of the territory ever since. The Polisario Front, the Sahrawi independence movement backed by Algeria, still demands that vote. For the UN, Western Sahara remains a non-self-governing territory awaiting decolonisation.

For decades, Madrid tried to keep its distance from its own unfinished business. That balance broke in 2021. In April, Spain allowed the Polisario leader, Brahim Ghali, to be treated for Covid in a hospital in Logroño. Weeks later, around 10,000 people crossed into Ceuta in two days, many swimming around the breakwaters, while Moroccan border guards looked on. Rabat recalled its ambassador. Madrid called it blackmail.

The reconciliation came at a price. In March 2022, Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez sent King Mohammed VI a letter describing Morocco's autonomy plan, which keeps the territory under Moroccan sovereignty, as "the most serious, realistic and credible basis" for a solution. It was the biggest shift in Spain's position in almost half a century. Rabat sent its ambassador back. Algeria, Spain's main supplier of natural gas, suspended a twenty-year friendship treaty and froze most trade with Spain. Sánchez's coalition partners and the opposition both criticised him for changing policy without consulting parliament.

## A neighbour too important to lose

Spain's reasons are easier to understand from Tarifa. Morocco is Spain's main trading partner in Africa and Spain is Morocco's largest trading partner. Rabat controls the departure points of one of the main migration routes into Europe. Cooperation on irregular migration and counter-terrorism depends on good relations. The two countries will also co-host the 2030 World Cup with Portugal.

Morocco, for its part, has turned the Sahara into the yardstick of all its foreign relations. In a 2022 speech, the king called it "the prism through which Morocco views its international environment". Since then, France, the United Kingdom and a growing number of countries have followed Spain in backing the autonomy plan. The United States has recognised Moroccan sovereignty outright since 2020.

The courts have been less accommodating. In October 2024, the Court of Justice of the European Union annulled the fisheries and agricultural trade deals between the EU and Morocco as applied to Western Sahara, because the Sahrawi people had not given their consent. The ruling hit Spanish fishermen from Andalusia and the Canary Islands, who depend on those waters.

## Without the UN

This autumn the dispute changed again. On 1 November, the UN mission that had monitored the ceasefire since 1991 shut down. The ceasefire itself had already collapsed in 2020. Its mandate expired after the United States and Russia vetoed each other's draft resolutions in the Security Council. Washington wanted to drop the reference to a referendum, the R in its name, MINURSO, and Moscow refused. Rabat called the outcome a step towards closing the file. Algiers expressed its "deep regret". Guinea's *Le Djely* warned that Morocco and Algeria could now come face to face.

For Spain, the former colonial power, the timing is awkward. It backs Morocco's plan but buys much of its gas from Algeria. And the bond with the Sahrawis has never quite broken: many in the Tindouf camps still speak Spanish, and Spanish families host thousands of Sahrawi children every summer through the *Vacaciones en Paz* programme.

From the scenic road above Tarifa, the two shores look close enough to touch. Fourteen kilometres of sea. Half a century of history that neither Madrid nor Rabat has managed to close.
