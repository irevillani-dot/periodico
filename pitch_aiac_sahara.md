**Subject:** Pitch: Western Sahara after MINURSO, a ceasefire without witnesses (≈800 words)

Dear editors,

On 31 October, the UN mission in Western Sahara expired after 35 years. It did not end because the conflict was solved. It ended because Washington and Moscow vetoed each other’s drafts in the Security Council, fighting over a single letter: the R of “referendum” in MINURSO. The US wanted it gone, to anchor negotiations in Morocco’s autonomy plan. Russia, speaking for Algeria, refused. The result was no mission at all.

I would like to write an 800-word piece on what this means for the Sahrawis, who were barely mentioned in New York.

**Why now.** The withdrawal of the blue helmets runs until January. On 10 November, the Polisario declared a general mobilisation against Moroccan troops massing along the sand wall. Rabat’s ambassador told the General Assembly the territory is “even more Moroccan” after the vote. Morocco’s next goal is diplomatic: to have Western Sahara struck off the UN list of non-self-governing territories, where it has sat since 1963.

**The angle.** Most coverage has treated the vote as a US–Russia or Morocco–Algeria story. I want to look at it from the territory itself:
- what the end of the mission removes on the ground, a buffer zone along the wall and the only international presence there, in a mission that never had a human rights mandate, while the UN human rights office has been kept out since 2015;
- how African and Maghreb media read the vote, from Guinea’s *Le Djely*, which warned of a direct Rabat–Algiers clash, to the duelling Moroccan and Algerian press;
- the role of Spain, the former colonial power, which backed Morocco’s plan in 2022 and goes to the polls on 29 November.

I would build on, not repeat, AIAC’s recent coverage, including Eoghan Gilmartin’s piece on *The Odyssey* filming in Dakhla. I would use it as a closing image of how “normalisation” proceeds even as the UN leaves.
